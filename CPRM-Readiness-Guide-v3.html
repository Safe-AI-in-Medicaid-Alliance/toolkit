How to Use This Readiness Guide

This guide is organized as a state-ready implementation resource. It can be used as:

  • A writing base for the final SAMA deliverable
  • A readiness checklist for state agencies at any maturity stage
  • A source for a short MESC executive summary
  • A framework to intake state-specific inputs and priorities
  • A reference for procurement staff evaluating vendor AI claims
Key Takeaway: The safest path is not to buy an AI product and search for a problem. The safest path is to define the Medicaid decision, govern the workflow, validate the data and model, train the humans, and scale only when outputs are useful, fair, explainable, and defensible.

Executive Summary

State Medicaid agencies face a practical problem: they are expected to protect public funds, detect fraud, waste, and abuse, support federal and state integrity priorities, and maintain access to care for eligible members — including rural and underserved communities — with finite staffing and uneven data quality.

AI and advanced analytics can help states move from episodic review toward continuous provider risk monitoring. The value is not replacing investigators or Medicaid leadership. The value is using analytics to organize large volumes of claims, enrollment, documentation, ownership, access, and public-source information into higher-quality leads, clearer risk tiers, and more defensible evidence packages for human review.

What good looks like: A state with effective continuous monitoring generates fewer but higher-quality leads, resolves cases faster, documents decisions defensibly, avoids disrupting rural access, and can explain its process to any audience — from legislators to ALJs to providers. The technology serves the mission; the mission does not serve the technology.

Central Premise: States should not ask, "Can AI find fraud?" in isolation. They should ask, "Can this specific AI-enabled workflow help us prioritize the right risks, protect legitimate providers and beneficiaries, document our reasoning, and withstand operational, legal, and equity review?"

1. The Problem

1.1 Static provider oversight is no longer enough ▼

Provider enrollment, screening, and revalidation remain the front door for program integrity, but revalidation alone does not identify all fraud, waste, or abuse. A provider may pass every enrollment rule and still present risk because of the services billed, the entities connected to the provider, billing velocity, documentation patterns, referral networks, or sudden changes in service mix.

Continuous provider risk monitoring fills this gap by creating an ongoing cycle: collect signals → stratify risk → prioritize human review → document evidence → act proportionately → feed outcomes back into the monitoring system.

1.2 Lead quality matters more than lead volume ▼

Many states do not lack leads. They lack staff capacity to pursue every lead, and they need better ways to select the leads most likely to produce a viable outcome. The goal is not to maximize flags. The goal is to generate fewer, better, more explainable, more defensible leads.

  • Some states must focus on cases with the greatest recovery potential; others may prioritize patient harm or network risk.
  • Some states may be prohibited from using extrapolation and need tools to identify which claims deserve human attention.
  • Some states are more focused on prevention and enrollment; others on post-payment investigations.
  • All states need transparency about why a lead was generated and what evidence exists before taking action.
1.3 Rural access and program integrity are connected ▼

Program integrity cannot be treated only as a recovery function. In rural areas, a single provider may be critical to maintaining access. A poorly calibrated risk program can unintentionally burden or drive out legitimate providers.

Continuous provider risk monitoring should include access guardrails. Before escalating adverse action, the workflow should ask:

  • What access gap would this action create?
  • Are there alternative providers?
  • Are members likely to experience travel, delay, or continuity-of-care disruption?
  • Is the state dealing with fraud vs. a good-faith provider who needs education?
1.4 AI changes what states can see — and must govern ▼

AI can analyze information previously difficult to use at scale: multi-page clinical records, call recordings, consent artifacts, ownership networks, provider websites, service patterns, geospatial shifts, and longitudinal behavior. That expanded visibility can help identify hidden risk earlier — but also creates obligations: states must understand model purpose, data sources, limitations, bias risks, explainability, audit trail, and decision rights before relying on the output.

1.5 Why this matters now ▼

States are under increasing pressure to improve program integrity without creating unnecessary burden for providers or barriers for members. Fraud schemes can move faster than traditional audits. Public data can be incomplete or misleading. Vendors are bringing AI products into Medicaid environments, and states need a practical way to ask the right questions, monitor performance, and ensure tools remain safe over time.

Implementation Warning: An AI-generated risk score, without governance, workflow integration, evidence standards, and human review, is not a program integrity tool. It is an unmanaged liability.

Use Case 1: Billing Pattern Anomaly Detection (Lead Generation) Conventional Analytics ML

Data Inputs

Paid claims data (procedure codes, dates, units, amounts), provider peer group benchmarks, historical billing patterns for same provider

What AI Does

Clustering and statistical outlier detection (techniques states have used for years, now enhanced with machine learning): identifies providers billing at volumes or patterns significantly different from peers in same taxonomy/geography.

Expected Output

Prioritized lead list with: provider ID, risk score, top 3 contributing factors (e.g., "billing 4x peer average for code X"), confidence level, and recommended next step (desk review, record request, etc.)

Workflow Impact

Feeds into Triage (Step 4). Investigators receive fewer but higher-quality leads with pre-assembled context instead of raw data dumps.

What good looks like: Investigators spend less time sorting through data and more time on substantive review. Lead-to-action conversion rates improve because leads arrive with context and evidence already assembled.

Use Case 2: Provider Network / Ownership Graph Analysis Machine Learning

Data Inputs

Provider enrollment data (ownership, managing employees, TINs), corporate registration records, shared addresses/phone numbers, NPI/NPPES data, known sanction lists

What AI Does

Graph analysis: maps relationships between providers, identifies connected entities, surfaces hidden ownership links. Flags networks where one entity has sanctions/fraud history and others share ownership or management.

Expected Output

Network visualization showing provider relationships, risk inheritance paths, and flagged connections. Summary: "Provider A (sanctioned) shares managing employee with Providers B, C (active, no prior flags)."

Workflow Impact

Feeds into Risk Signal Generation (Step 2) and Investigation (Step 5). Enables proactive enrollment screening and supports network-level investigations.

Use Case 3: Document Summarization & Triage Generative AI

Data Inputs

Clinical records, treatment plans, progress notes, transportation logs, consent forms, prior authorization documentation

What AI Does

NLP/LLM summarization: extracts key facts from multi-page records, identifies missing documentation, flags inconsistencies between billed services and clinical notes. Cites source pages.

Expected Output

Structured summary per provider/claim set: services billed vs. documented, missing authorizations, templated/duplicate note alerts, with page citations. Confidence flags for uncertain extractions.

Workflow Impact

Feeds into Investigation/Review (Step 5). Reduces auditor time per case from hours to minutes for initial document triage. Human auditor still makes final determination.

What good looks like: An auditor who previously spent 3–4 hours reviewing a 200-page clinical record can now review an AI-generated summary in 20 minutes, then spot-check the source pages cited. Throughput increases without sacrificing quality.

Use Case 4: Cross-State Provider Visibility Machine Learning

Data Inputs

CMS public provider data sets, NPPES registry, OIG exclusion lists, state-reported sanctions, multi-state provider enrollment records, Provider Network Directory data

What AI Does

Entity resolution and cross-referencing: identifies providers operating across multiple states, flags cases where a provider has been sanctioned or investigated in one state but remains active in others. Matches on NPI, name variants, TIN, address proximity.

Expected Output

Alert: "Provider X (NPI: ###) terminated in State A for [reason] on [date] — currently active in State B, C with $X in annual billings." Includes linked entity details and source citations.

Workflow Impact

Feeds into Signal Capture (Step 1) and Enrollment Risk Tiering. Enables states to catch risks that would be invisible without cross-program visibility.

Use Case 5: Enrollment/Revalidation Risk Tiering Conventional Analytics ML

Data Inputs

Provider application data, ownership disclosures, prior investigation history, peer group risk indicators, geographic/access context

What AI Does

Risk scoring: compares provider attributes against known risk indicators. Assigns risk tier (standard, enhanced, deny-pending-review) based on combination of factors. Explains contributing features.

Expected Output

Risk tier assignment with rationale: "Enhanced review recommended — 3 risk factors: new entity formed <6 months, shared address with previously terminated provider, high-risk service category."

Workflow Impact

Front-door prevention. Enrollment staff use tier assignment to determine review depth. Tool does NOT deny enrollment — it routes applications for appropriate human review level.

Use Case 6: Rural Access Impact Overlay Conventional Analytics

Data Inputs

Provider locations, member locations, travel time data, network adequacy standards, provider taxonomy/capacity, risk signals from other use cases

What AI Does

Geospatial analysis: maps provider risk signals against geographic access data. Calculates impact of potential adverse actions on member access. Identifies sole-community providers.

Expected Output

Access impact report: "If Provider Y is suspended, 1,200 members in County Z would need to travel 45+ additional miles for behavioral health services. Next nearest provider at capacity."

Workflow Impact

Feeds into Access & Proportionality Review (Step 6). Ensures states consider access consequences before taking high-consequence integrity actions in rural/underserved areas.

Note: These use cases are illustrative. Each state should define use cases based on their specific priorities, data availability, legal authority, and staff capacity. The model card template (§9.3) — a standardized disclosure form for an AI tool, similar to a nutrition label — provides a structured way to document each use case.

3. The Solution: 4-Phase AI-Enabled Model

Each phase has a decision gate so states do not scale a tool before governance, workflow, evidence, and access safeguards are ready. Phases can be compressed or expanded based on state readiness.

Phase Overview

PhaseTimingPrimary QuestionDecision Gate
Phase 1 Foundation Months 0–2 Are we ready to pilot safely? Proceed only if the state can define purpose, governance, data, and safeguards
Phase 2 Pilots Months 3–6 Can this workflow improve lead quality without harm? Proceed only if pilot shows useful performance without unacceptable bias or burden
Phase 3 Scale Months 6–12 Can we integrate and operate across workflows? Proceed only if operational capacity, monitoring, and vendor controls are ready
Phase 4 Continuous Ops Month 12+ Can we sustain, monitor, and improve responsibly? Continue only if benefits, fairness, access, and defensibility are maintained

4. The Workflow: Human-Centered and Auditable

AI is used to support risk monitoring, not to make final adverse decisions. The workflow should be adapted to state-specific context: prevention, enrollment, post-payment review, payment hold, provider education, audit, investigation, referral, contested case hearing, or access monitoring.
What good looks like: A state running this workflow can reconstruct the full decision path for any case — from the original data signal through human review to final action — and explain it in plain language to an ALJ, auditor, legislator, or provider within 15 minutes. Every step has an owner, a timestamp, and documented rationale.

4.1 Human-in-the-Loop Operating Model

1

Signal Capture

AI role: Ingest and normalize claims, enrollment, documentation, ownership, and public data (including CMS data sets and cross-state sources)

Human role: Data owners confirm source authority, quality, and permitted uses

Output: Data source register and data quality scorecard

2

Risk Signal Generation

AI role: Identify anomalies, peer outliers, text/document patterns, ownership changes, cross-state flags, and behavioral shifts

Human role: SMEs validate whether signals are meaningful and exclude inappropriate features

Output: Signal catalog with rationale and exclusion list

3

Risk Stratification

AI role: Score or tier provider risk using rules, models, or hybrid approaches

Human role: Program leaders approve thresholds based on capacity, priorities, and legal requirements

Output: Risk tier, top contributing factors, confidence level, and limitations

4

Triage

AI role: Organize alerts by priority and generate supporting evidence summaries

Human role: Triage team accepts, rejects, routes, or requests more information

Output: Triage decision, reason code, reviewer, and timestamp

5

Investigation / Review

AI role: Organize records, highlight relevant documents, identify related providers or claims

Human role: Investigator/auditor conducts independent review and documents rationale

Output: Evidence packet with human analysis and independent findings

6

Access & Proportionality Review

AI role: Provide geographic, network, and access context for the provider

Human role: Access/policy staff assess rural impact and proportionality

Output: Access impact assessment and mitigation plan (if needed)

7

Action Decision

AI role: Provide organized evidence and contributing factors for decision-maker

Human role: Authorized official approves action with documented rationale and legal authority

Output: Action decision, authority cited, approval chain, and provider notice

8

Feedback & Learning

AI role: Ingest dispositions, appeal outcomes, and reviewer feedback

Human role: Data/model team evaluates performance, bias, and improvement opportunities

Output: Performance report, model recalibration input, and lessons learned

Defensibility Design Principle: At every step, the state should be able to explain — in plain language to an ALJ, auditor, or legislator — what data was used, what the AI did, what the human decided, and why. If you cannot reconstruct that path, the workflow is not ready for high-consequence actions.

5. Governance Rules

Governance is not a separate compliance exercise. It is the set of rules that makes AI-enabled risk monitoring usable, defensible, and sustainable.

Prioritization guidance: Twelve rules can feel like twelve prerequisites. They are not. Four rules are marked REQUIRED BEFORE PILOT — these are non-negotiable before any live testing. The remaining eight should be BUILT OVER FIRST YEAR as the program matures.

1. Define the use case before selecting a tool REQUIRED BEFORE PILOT

Every AI-enabled capability should have a written use-case charter that identifies the business problem, decision supported, affected populations, data sources, human decision rights, risks, success metrics, and stop conditions.

2. No autonomous adverse action REQUIRED BEFORE PILOT

AI-generated outputs may prioritize, summarize, recommend, or route. They should not independently deny enrollment, terminate providers, reduce access, initiate recovery, or make eligibility decisions without state-approved human review and due process.

3. Preserve evidence lineage REQUIRED BEFORE PILOT

The state must be able to show where data came from, how it was transformed, why the alert fired, what model/version was used, what the human reviewer did, and what evidence supported action.

4. Treat data quality as a risk control BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

Public data, provider location data, NPI/NPPES records, billing locations, and service locations may be incomplete or misleading. States should validate data before drawing conclusions and should document known limitations.

5. Test for bias and proxy discrimination BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

Models should not learn that geography, ethnicity, language, community, or other protected/proxy characteristics are risk indicators when the true signal is service pattern, ownership, billing behavior, documentation, or referral structure.

6. Calibrate thresholds to capacity and law BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

Each state should choose thresholds that align with staff capacity, recovery priorities, patient harm risks, network adequacy, and statutory constraints. More alerts are not always better.

7. Monitor provider burden and rural access BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

Before escalation, assess whether action would disrupt access, especially in rural areas or provider-scarce service lines. Consider education, corrective action, or targeted review when appropriate.

8. Require vendor transparency and accountability BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

Vendors must document data sources, model behavior, updates, explainability, testing, bias controls, security, subcontractors, audit logs, data rights, and transition/exit procedures. For multi-layer AI solutions, the primary vendor remains liable and must disclose the full technology stack.

9. Design for hearings, audits, and public scrutiny BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

Assume the state may need to explain the system to an ALJ, auditor, legislator, provider, beneficiary, journalist, or public-records requester. Plain-language documentation should exist before the first high-consequence action.

10. Keep humans accountable and empowered REQUIRED BEFORE PILOT

Human reviewers must be trained, must have time to review the evidence, must be able to override the tool, and must document their independent rationale.

11. Control vendor use of state data for AI training BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

Vendor solutions should not ingest state or provider data as training data unless the state explicitly determines that usage is appropriate through a documented process. Data used for training must be de-identified per state and federal standards.

12. Require transparency and disclosure via model cards BUILD OVER FIRST YEAR

States should require vendors to provide standardized model documentation (model cards — a standardized disclosure form for an AI tool, similar to a nutrition label) covering: intended use, performance metrics, training data, quantitative analysis, ethical considerations, and limitations.

5.2 Provider Disclosure: Should States Tell Providers AI Is Used?NEW

States will be asked whether providers are informed that AI-assisted tools are part of risk monitoring. This guide recommends states develop a disclosure position early, because the question will arise from provider associations, legislators, and the press regardless.

Considerations:

  • Transparency builds trust: Providers who understand how monitoring works are less likely to perceive it as arbitrary or punitive.
  • Disclosure does not mean revealing methodology: States can describe the general approach (e.g., "we use analytics to identify billing patterns for human review") without disclosing specific thresholds or detection logic.
  • Legal considerations vary: Some states may have open-records obligations that effectively require disclosure; others may have investigatory exemptions. Legal counsel should advise.
  • Timing matters: Disclosure at enrollment/revalidation (general notice) differs from disclosure during an active investigation (specific notice with due process implications).

At minimum, states should document their disclosure policy and ensure it is consistent across communications. Silence is not a strategy — it becomes the story when a provider or journalist asks.

This framework helps states understand how AI affects existing program integrity risks. For each conventional risk, we identify how AI may expand exposure and what AI-specific mitigations are needed.

How to use this framework: For each AI use case the state is considering, walk through these risks and document which mitigations are in place, which are planned, and which gaps remain. This becomes the basis for the governance board's risk acceptance decision at each phase gate.

8.4 Funding PathwaysNEW

"Funding or federal/state approval uncertainty" was cited as a top-3 barrier by nearly half of survey respondents. This subsection outlines common funding vehicles for AI-enabled program integrity work.

Federal Match Rates

  • 90/10 federal match (Design, Development, Installation): Available for MES investments including AI/analytics systems during initial development, subject to CMS APD approval. Covers design through go-live.
  • 75/25 federal match (Operations): Available for ongoing operation and maintenance of approved MES systems, including AI monitoring tools after go-live.
  • 50/50 federal match (Administrative): May apply to staff activities, training, and governance functions that support program integrity but are not part of an approved MES project.

APD (Advance Planning Document) Submissions

States funding AI through MES channels will typically need to submit an APD to CMS. Key elements for an AI-focused APD include:

  • Clear problem statement tied to program integrity outcomes
  • Use-case definition with expected ROI and success metrics
  • Governance framework demonstrating responsible AI use
  • Staffing and training plan
  • Cost-benefit analysis with phased implementation timeline
  • Security, privacy, and data governance documentation

Peer reference: At least one participating state (Colorado) has expressed interest in sharing APD approaches with peers. The workstream will facilitate these exchanges where states consent.

Other Funding Vehicles

  • MFCU coordination: Joint investments with the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit where analytics support both PI and MFCU investigations
  • Existing MMIS/MES contracts: Adding AI modules to existing vendor contracts through change orders or option periods
  • State general funds: For smaller pilots or governance activities that do not meet MES APD thresholds
  • CMS Medicaid Innovation Accelerator Program (IAP): Technical assistance resources that may offset state costs for planning and design

9.2 Vendor and Product Questions

Use these questions during procurement, product evaluation, and ongoing vendor oversight:

  • What specific decision or workflow does the product support, and what decisions does it not make?
  • What data sources, fields, and refresh cycles are required? Which data leave the state environment?
  • Can the vendor provide a model card covering: intended use, performance metrics, training data, quantitative analysis, ethical considerations, and known limitations?
  • Does the vendor use third-party AI models (e.g., foundation models from AWS, OpenAI, Anthropic)? If so, provide an implementation fact sheet.
  • What is the vendor's policy on using state data, provider data, or prompt data for training their models? Can the state opt out?
  • How are outputs explained to investigators, enrollment staff, legal/hearings staff, providers, and non-technical executives?
  • How does the product detect or mitigate bias, proxy variables, model drift, data quality problems, and hallucinated or unsupported summaries?
  • Can the state access audit logs showing input data, model version, run date, output, reviewer actions, overrides, and dispositions?
  • How will the vendor support contested cases, audits, public-records requests, legislative inquiries, and independent validation?
  • What PHI/PII protections, security controls, subcontractor disclosures, and data retention/deletion procedures apply?
  • What happens if the state terminates the contract? How are data, models, configurations, and documentation returned or destroyed?
  • What are the total cost components: licensing, compute/inference, data storage, integration, support, and annual cost trajectory?

9.5 ALJ-Ready Evidence Packet Checklist

Ensure each evidence packet can withstand review by an administrative law judge:

  • Plain-language case summary and action requested
  • Statutory/regulatory/policy authority for action
  • Provider identity, ownership, enrollment, taxonomy, and location history
  • Claims or records at issue, with source references and dates
  • Explanation of how the lead was generated, including AI/analytics role and limitations
  • Model version, data refresh date, threshold, and top contributing factors
  • Human reviewer analysis and independent rationale (separate from AI output)
  • Provider communications, notices, responses, and opportunities to cure or contest
  • Access and rural impact review, where relevant
  • Disposition recommendation and supervisor/legal approval

9.6 Rural Access Impact Review Checklist

Complete before any high-consequence action against a provider in a potentially access-sensitive area:

  • Is the provider the only or one of few providers for the service in a county, region, tribal area, frontier area, or rural community?
  • What is the current member travel time/distance, and how would it change if the provider is limited, suspended, or terminated?
  • Are alternative providers accepting Medicaid members and available within clinically appropriate timeframes?
  • Would provider education, a corrective action plan, or staged intervention protect integrity while maintaining access?
  • Is there evidence of patient harm or exploitation that requires immediate action despite access concerns?
  • Has the state documented an access mitigation plan before high-consequence action?

10. Peer Case StudiesNEW

Peer state examples were the #1 requested resource in the SAMA survey (5 of 7 respondents). The following anonymized vignettes illustrate real implementation patterns — one success, one near-miss that produced important lessons.

Vignette A: Document Triage Pilot — Success

State profile: Mid-size state, ~2M Medicaid enrollees, existing MMIS with limited analytics capability.

Problem: Program integrity staff were reviewing an average of 180 pages of clinical documentation per case, spending 3–4 hours per provider on initial triage. Backlog was growing by ~20 cases/month.

What they did: Piloted an LLM-based document summarization tool (Use Case 3) on a subset of post-payment review cases. Limited scope to behavioral health claims where documentation volume was highest. Required human auditor to validate every AI-generated summary against source documents for the first 60 days.

Results after 6 months:

  • Initial triage time reduced from 3.5 hours to ~45 minutes per case
  • Backlog eliminated within 4 months
  • Auditor override rate: 12% (AI summary missed relevant context); this dropped to 6% after prompt refinement
  • No cases where AI-only summary would have led to incorrect adverse action (human review caught all gaps)
  • Total pilot cost: ~$85K (vendor licensing + staff time for validation)

Key lesson: Starting with a narrow, well-defined use case where the AI assists rather than decides made governance straightforward. The 60-day parallel validation period built staff trust and identified prompt improvements before scaling.

Vignette B: Risk Scoring Without Access Review — Near-Miss

State profile: Large state, federated Medicaid structure, vendor-built risk scoring tool deployed in production.

Problem: The state deployed a provider risk scoring tool that identified statistical outliers for enhanced review. The tool performed well on accuracy metrics in urban areas.

What went wrong: In a rural county, the tool flagged the only pediatric behavioral health provider within 90 miles. The provider's billing was an outlier because they were the sole provider — all members in the county used them, creating volume patterns that looked anomalous compared to urban peers. The case was routed to prepayment review hold.

Impact (caught before harm): A provider relations staff member noticed the hold during a routine check and escalated. The hold was released within 5 days, but the provider had already begun turning away new patients due to cash flow concerns. Members reported delayed appointments.

What they changed:

  • Added mandatory Rural Access Impact Overlay (Use Case 6) as a pre-action check
  • Recalibrated peer groups to account for sole-community provider status
  • Added "access flag" to dashboard so triage staff see geographic context immediately
  • Established escalation policy: any action against a sole-community provider requires director-level review

Key lesson: A model can be accurate on its own terms and still create harm if the workflow doesn't account for context the model cannot see. Access review is not optional — it must be structural.

Note: These vignettes are composites drawn from workstream discussions and public enforcement patterns. They do not represent any single state's experience. The workstream welcomes additional case studies from participating states (with state consent) for future versions.

11. Talking Points: How to Describe AI-Assisted Risk MonitoringNEW

The readiness guide repeatedly notes that states should "assume you'll explain this to a legislator, ALJ, journalist, or provider." Here is starter language for those conversations. Adapt to your state's context, legal framework, and communication norms.

For the Legislature / Oversight Committees

"We are using data analytics — including some AI-assisted tools — to help our program integrity staff focus on the providers and claims most likely to represent a genuine risk to the program or to patients. These tools do not make decisions. They organize information so that trained human reviewers can make better, faster, more documented decisions. We have governance rules in place, we test for bias, we protect rural access, and we can explain how any case was identified and reviewed. The goal is fewer, better investigations — not more flags."

For Provider Associations

"Our risk monitoring program uses analytics to identify unusual patterns — things like billing volumes significantly different from peers, or missing documentation. When patterns are flagged, a trained staff member reviews the case before any action is taken. No provider will face adverse action based solely on an algorithm. We include access safeguards so that legitimate providers — especially in rural or underserved areas — are not burdened by the monitoring process itself. We welcome provider feedback on how the process is experienced."

For the Press / Public Inquiries

"[State Agency] uses data analytics as one tool among many to protect Medicaid funds and the members who depend on them. Our approach is human-centered: technology helps us organize and prioritize information, but trained staff make all decisions about whether to take action. We have published governance principles, we test our tools for fairness, and providers have full due-process rights including notice and the ability to contest any finding. We monitor access to care so that our integrity work does not create barriers for members, particularly in rural communities."

Tips for all audiences: Lead with the human decision-maker. Name the safeguards. Acknowledge limitations. Offer specifics when asked. Avoid jargon (say "data analytics" rather than "machine learning" or "AI" unless the audience is technical). Never overstate what the tool does.

12. GlossaryNEW

Technical terms used in this guide, defined in plain language for non-technical readers.

TermPlain-Language Definition
ClusteringA method of grouping similar things together based on their characteristics. In this context: grouping providers with similar billing patterns so outliers become visible.
Disparate impactWhen a policy or tool affects one group disproportionately compared to others, even if that was not the intent. Example: a risk model that flags rural providers at higher rates than urban providers because of volume differences, not actual fraud.
Drift (model drift)When an AI model's performance degrades over time because the real world has changed since the model was built. Like a weather forecast model trained on summer data trying to predict winter storms.
Entity resolutionThe process of determining whether two records refer to the same real-world entity (person, organization) when they appear differently in different data sources. Example: matching "Dr. J. Smith, NPI 123" in one state with "John Smith MD, NPI 123" in another.
F1 scoreA single number that balances two concerns: how often the tool is right when it flags something (precision) and how often it catches things it should flag (recall). Higher is better; 1.0 would be perfect.
Foundation modelA large pre-trained AI model (like GPT or Claude) that can be adapted for specific tasks. Think of it as a general-purpose engine that vendors customize for Medicaid-specific work.
HallucinationWhen an AI generates information that sounds plausible but is not supported by the source data. In document summarization: stating a clinical note contains something it does not actually say.
MLOps / ModelOpsThe practices and tools for managing AI models in production — version control, monitoring, updating, and retiring models. Similar to how IT manages software releases.
Model cardA standardized disclosure form for an AI tool, similar to a nutrition label. Documents what the model does, how it performs, what data it was trained on, known limitations, and who should review its outputs.
NLP (Natural Language Processing)Technology that enables computers to read and interpret human language — clinical notes, narrative reports, provider communications.
LLM (Large Language Model)A type of AI trained on large amounts of text that can summarize, generate, and analyze written content. GPT, Claude, and similar systems are LLMs.
PrecisionOf all the things the tool flagged, how many were actually problems? High precision means fewer false alarms.
Proxy variableA data point that stands in for something else. Example: ZIP code as a proxy for race or income. Models can learn proxy discrimination without explicitly using protected characteristics.
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)A technique where an AI looks up relevant documents before generating a response, rather than relying only on its training. Helps ground outputs in actual source material and reduces hallucination.
RecallOf all the actual problems that exist, how many did the tool catch? High recall means fewer missed cases.
Unsupervised learningAI that finds patterns in data without being told what "right" and "wrong" look like in advance. Useful when historical labels may carry bias (e.g., past investigation patterns may reflect enforcement priorities rather than actual fraud distribution).
